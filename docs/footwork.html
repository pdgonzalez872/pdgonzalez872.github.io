Movement

Footwork

Drills for moving well on a tennis or padel court, all of them doable alone, most of them in a few minutes. There is a section at the end for running the same qualities as games with kids. Measure first, then drill. Videos play right here on the page.

The focus

  • This is not about running faster. It is about arriving early and arriving balanced. Almost every bad shot at club level is a good swing taken from a bad place.
  • Three qualities, in the order they actually matter:
    • Stopping. Deceleration is where balance is lost. Nobody practices it and everybody needs it.
    • The split step. Landing as the ball is struck, not before and not after.
    • Recovery. The steps after the shot, which decide whether the next ball is playable at all.
  • Measure before you drill. Four tests at the bottom of this section. Without a number, six weeks from now you will have a feeling instead of an answer, and feelings about your own movement are unreliable in exactly one direction.
  • Fifteen minutes, twice a week, attached to court time you already have. A separate session is a session that gets skipped.

The idea

Good movement is mostly good stopping

Watch someone who moves beautifully and the striking thing is not the acceleration. It is that they seem to arrive and settle in one motion, already turned, already balanced, with time to spare. They are not faster than you over five metres. They stop better, so they can afford to leave earlier.

That reframes the whole training problem. Sprint work makes you faster in a straight line, which is close to useless on a court that is ten metres wide. What you want is the ability to hit the brakes on one leg, hold that position without wobbling, and push off from it in a new direction. That is a strength and balance quality, and it responds fast, which is the good news at 38.

The second thing, and it is unglamorous: the split step is a timing skill, not a fitness one. Most people do it, just early. They land, stall, and then have to restart from a dead stop. Landing on the strike is the whole trick, and it can be practiced in a hallway with no ball.

The four tests, run once before any of this

  • balance Single-leg stance, eyes closed, each leg. Record seconds until you have to put a foot down.
  • agility Hexagon test. Both directions, record seconds.
  • change of dir 5-10-5 shuttle. Record both first-step directions separately; the asymmetry is often the finding.
  • on court Spider run. Five balls placed around the court, one at a time back to the centre, timed.

Twenty-five minutes, a phone timer, no equipment beyond what is in the bag. Repeat identically at the end. Identically matters more than precisely: same surface, same shoes, same time of day, same warm-up.

The drills, all solo

Stopping and landing

The one that matters most and gets trained least. Every rep is judged on silence: if the landing makes noise, you absorbed it with the floor instead of with your leg.

  1. Snap down3 × 5

    Small hop, land in an athletic stance, stick it dead. No second adjustment step.

  2. Single-leg snap down3 × 5 each

    Same thing on one leg. Knee tracks over the toe, hold two seconds before the next rep.

  3. Skater bound and stick3 × 6 each

    Bound sideways, land on the outside leg, hold. This is the exact shape of a wide ball recovery.

  4. Drop step and brake3 × 6 each

    Three hard steps to one side, then stop in two steps instead of four. Count the steps out loud.

Balance and single-leg stability

Boring, invisible, and the thing that decides whether a stretched ball is a shot or a scramble. Do it barefoot when you can.

  1. Single-leg stance, eyes closed3 × 30s each

    Eyes open is a different exercise and a much easier one. Closed is the point.

  2. Single-leg RDL reach3 × 8 each

    No weight. Hinge at the hip, back leg straight, reach the floor, return without touching down.

  3. Clock reach2 × 5 each

    Stand on one leg, reach the free foot to three points on an imaginary clock face without shifting the standing foot.

  4. Single-leg calf raise2 × 15 each

    Slow down, three seconds. The ankle is the first thing that gives out in the fifth set.

First step and split step

Timing work. Needs no court, no ball, and no space. A hallway is enough.

  1. Split step on a cue3 × 10

    Have something external trigger it: a metronome, a video of a rally, someone clapping. Land on the cue, not before it. Practicing it alone in silence teaches you to land early, which is the exact fault you are trying to fix.

  2. Split and go3 × 8

    Split, then explode two steps in a direction called at random. Randomness matters; a known direction is a different skill.

  3. Lateral shuffle and crossover3 × 6 each

    Three shuffle steps out, then crossover step to recover. The crossover is what covers ground; the shuffle is what keeps you square.

  4. Hexagon3 × 2 circuits

    Two-footed jumps in and out of a hexagon, both directions. Also one of the four tests, so it doubles as calibration.

Tennis specific

Court needed, ball optional.

  1. Spider run3 reps

    Five balls placed around the court, retrieved one at a time to the centre. Full recovery between reps, otherwise it becomes a conditioning drill and stops measuring movement.

  2. Recovery to centre3 × 6 each

    Start wide in a corner, shadow the shot, then recover with crossover steps and split at the centre. The split at the end is not optional; that is the rep.

  3. Shadow swings with full footwork5 × 30s

    No ball. Forces you to notice the steps, because there is nothing else to pay attention to.

  4. Serve plus first step3 × 8

    Serve, then the landing and the recovery step. Most of us practice the serve and stop the rep at contact, which is the half that never happens in a match.

Padel specific

Different game, different feet. The walls change what good positioning even means.

  1. Step out from the glass3 × 8 each

    From near the back wall, step diagonally back and away rather than sideways, so the ball comes off the glass into space you have already made. Getting stuck against the glass is a footwork error, not a reading error.

  2. Bandeja footwork3 × 8 each

    Side-steps back while keeping the chest toward the net. Never fully turn; turning is how you end up hitting it from behind you.

  3. Net to back and return3 × 6

    Three shuffles back from the net, split, three forward. Padel is won and lost on this transition more than on any shot.

  4. Wall feed and reposition4 × 45s

    Feed yourself off the back wall, and after each contact reset to a defined position before the next one. The reset is the drill; the hit is incidental.

How to actually run it

Fifteen minutes, twice a week. Pick one from each of the first three blocks, then one sport-specific block. Rotate the sport block week to week.

  1. Warm up3 min

    Ankles and hips. Cold single-leg work is how people get hurt doing an injury prevention drill.

  2. Stopping4 min

    Always first, while you are fresh. Quality collapses when tired and a sloppy landing rep is worse than no rep.

  3. Split step or first step4 min
  4. Sport specific4 min
  5. Balancewhenever

    Single-leg stance costs nothing and needs no space. Brushing teeth, waiting for coffee. It does not need to live inside the session.

With kids

Same qualities, wearing a costume

Every drill above trains something a six year old also needs, and none of them survive contact with a six year old in the form written above. The fix is not to simplify them. It is to stop calling them drills, give each one a number, and make somebody win.

Two rules that matter more than the content. Every game has a count, even if the only opponent is your own last number. And stop while they still want more. Ten minutes that end early get asked for again; twenty minutes that end in boredom do not.

With a wide age gap, the younger one is often there to copy the older one rather than to do the thing properly, and that is not a failure mode, it is the mechanism. Copying an older sibling is the most efficient movement instruction available and it costs you nothing.

The circuit

Six games, ten to twelve minutes, no equipment beyond cones or anything cone shaped. Pick four.

  1. Statuesbalance

    Run around, and on the clap freeze on one foot. Last one standing wins. This is the single-leg stance drill and nobody notices.

  2. Red light with a jumpstopping

    Red light green light, but on red you have to land a two-footed jump and hold it still. The whole deceleration block, as a game.

  3. Line jumps against the clockfoot speed

    Jump side to side over a line for twenty seconds, count them. Next round, beat your own number. They will beat it. That is the point.

  4. Animal walkscoordination

    Crab walk out to a cone, bear crawl back. The one that works for a four year old with no instruction at all.

  5. Treasure collectionchange of dir

    Five balls in a fan, bring them back to the middle one at a time, timed. This is the spider run in a costume, and it works on anyone from four to forty.

  6. Mirrorreaction

    Face each other, one leads side to side, the other copies. Swap who leads. Ends in laughing every time, which is the actual reason it is on the list.

What to watch for

You are not coaching technique. You are looking for four things, and only one of them is about them.

  1. Do they stop under control?

    Not how fast they go. Whether they can stop where they meant to.

  2. Both sides?

    Kids build a strong side early and then hide the other one. Cheap to fix now, expensive later.

  3. Are they still upright when tired?

    The last thirty seconds tell you more than the first two minutes.

  4. Did they ask to do it again?

    The only metric that decides whether this exists in three months. If the answer is no three times running, the content is not the problem, the framing is. Change it or stop.
